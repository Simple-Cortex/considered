# Persuade-Mode Surfaces

Purpose: Build a specific, evidenced argument that earns attention, belief, and one honest commitment.

Contents: commitment, narrative arc, first viewport, proof, objections, social proof, pricing, CTAs, section test, portfolios, AI tells, audit.
The `PERS-` rules below are guidance rules: no linter emits them, and they surface only by reading this catalog at its routed stage.

## Commit to a sharp case
Choose the one belief that must become true before the visitor will act.
State the offer in language a real buyer would repeat to a colleague.
Make the consequence concrete: name the gain, relief, status, access, or change.
Choose a point of view strong enough to exclude the wrong audience.
Lead with the most consequential difference, not the broadest category claim.
Build an argument whose sequence feels inevitable in hindsight.
Use visual direction to amplify the case, not to decorate around it.
Make the product, work, or outcome visible as early as credibility permits.
Give every section one persuasion job and one measurable reason to exist.
End each section with more confidence than the visitor brought into it.
Define the conversion as a human decision before naming the button.
Write it in this form:

> A [specific visitor] decides to [commitment] because [promise] is credible through [proof], despite [primary objection].
Use that sentence to rank content.
Promote material that changes the decision.
Compress material that confirms an already settled point.
Remove material that neither raises belief nor lowers risk.

## Build the narrative arc
Arrange the page around the visitor's changing questions.
Treat the sequence below as an earning order, then adapt it to buying maturity.
Let high-intent visitors reach offer details early while the full argument remains available.

| Stage | Visitor question | Section job | What the section must earn |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Orientation | Is this for me? | Name the audience, consequence, and distinct promise | Earn the next five seconds |
| 2. Relevance | Do you understand my situation? | Show the recognized problem, desire, or standard | Earn self-identification |
| 3. Mechanism | How does this produce the result? | Explain the product, method, or creative logic | Earn plausibility |
| 4. Evidence | Has it worked in reality? | Present direct, specific, attributable proof | Earn belief |
| 5. Differentiation | Why choose this path? | Contrast meaningful alternatives on decision criteria | Earn preference |
| 6. Objection resolution | What could make this wrong for me? | Answer the highest-cost remaining doubt | Earn acceptable risk |
| 7. Offer | What exactly do I get, and on what terms? | Make scope, price, timing, and conditions legible | Earn decision readiness |
| 8. Commitment | What happens if I proceed? | Restate value, reduce procedural uncertainty, and present the action | Earn the click |
Start with the stage where the visitor's uncertainty is highest.
Move mechanism ahead of relevance when the category is already understood but the method is novel.
Move pricing ahead of extended proof when the visit is high intent and price determines fit.
Move a flagship case study into the opening when the work itself is the strongest argument.
Repeat proof at the exact points where confidence would otherwise fall.
Let the final commitment summarize the case rather than restart it.
### Give each transition a reason
Connect promise to mechanism with “here is how.”
Connect mechanism to proof with “here is what happened.”
Connect proof to differentiation with “here is why this result is not interchangeable.”
Connect differentiation to offer with “here is the choice now available.”
Connect offer to commitment with “here is the safe next step.”
Make the visitor feel progression, not scrolling.
### Keep the brakes short
Do not repeat the same claim in new visual wrappers.
Never use length as a substitute for a complete argument.

## Win the first viewport
Use the first viewport to establish orientation, consequence, credibility, and direction.
`PERS-04` (first viewport orients): make four things answerable without scrolling:

1. Name what this is in the visitor's language.
2. State the sharpest relevant promise.
3. Show one adjacent reason to believe.
4. Present the primary next step or a clear path into the argument.
Give the headline one job: make the consequential difference legible.
Use the supporting line to add mechanism, audience, or constraint.
Choose a visual that proves, demonstrates, or embodies the promise.
Show the real product when interaction is the differentiator.
Show the outcome when transformation is the differentiator.
Show the work when taste and authorship are the differentiators.
Show a precise result when measured performance is the differentiator.
Make navigation quiet enough for the proposition to win.
Let the first viewport contain air when the idea is singular.
Let it contain evidence density when scrutiny is high.
Keep the primary commitment visible without turning the viewport into a button field.
### Reserve the first viewport for earned material
Never place fabricated proof, invented logos, fake scarcity, or an unsupported superlative there.
Do not lead with a feature grid, a full FAQ, or a cluster of equal CTAs.
Do not spend the focal position on decorative art that cannot explain the offer.
Compose the first viewport as a deliberate frame, not an accidental crop: key material begins and completes inside it, nothing important is bisected by the fold, and the page passes judgment on its opening screenful alone — shipped pages are composed to the fold; scrolled-to excellence cannot rescue an unconsidered opening.

## Pair every claim with proof
`PERS-02` (claim-proof adjacency): never present a meaningful claim without adjacent evidence.
Treat adjacency as both spatial and cognitive.
Place proof in the same viewport, module, or immediate reading beat as the claim.
Match evidence strength to claim magnitude.
Make extraordinary promises carry unusually direct proof.
Use the shortest evidence form that resolves the doubt.
### Choose proof that fits the claim
Pair a performance claim with a measured result, baseline, period, and sample.
Pair a usability claim with a demonstration of the task.
Pair a quality claim with close inspection of the work or process.
Pair a trust claim with named accountability, terms, certifications, or operating history.
Pair a popularity claim with current, attributable adoption data.
Pair a transformation claim with a before-and-after case that names conditions.
Pair a differentiation claim with a legible comparison on criteria buyers value.
Pair a portfolio claim with the finished work, the brief, and the consequence of the decision.

`PERS-08` (proof shows the mechanism): when the brief supplies no proof asset for a claim, render the product mechanism itself as the evidence — a schema-faithful depiction built only from supplied fields, flows, or records (`HON-03`). Shipped landing pages sell with a product visual, not prose; the mechanism mock is the one proof that can always be shown without inventing a fact. Never leave the proof zone to prose alone when the mechanism can be drawn from supplied facts, and never let the mock smuggle in records, figures, or outcomes the facts do not supply.
### Compose evidence as content
Lead with the fact that changes belief.
Add attribution beside the fact.
Include timeframe, denominator, and conditions when they affect interpretation.
Let the visitor inspect the underlying case, source, or demonstration.
Use captions to explain why the evidence matters.
Turn screenshots into annotated demonstrations of the claimed behavior.
Show limits and fit boundaries to increase trust.
Separate observed results from projected outcomes.
### Protect credibility
`PERS-03` (evidence is real and attributable): never invent metrics, quotes, customers, awards, certifications, or logos.
Do not crop, stylize, or paraphrase evidence until its meaning changes.
Do not place proof so far away that the visitor must remember which claim it supports.
`PERS-03` covers evidence blocks; the general boundary is `HON-01`. A persuasion surface that invents its own proof context — a "before" condition nobody supplied, a definition that gives a supplied metric its meaning, a follow-up commitment in a success state (`HON-04`) — fails exactly like an invented logo. See the supplied-fact boundary in `craft.md` §4.7.

## Order objections by decision cost
`PERS-05` (objections ordered by decision cost) governs this section.
List objections before laying out the page.
Write each objection in the visitor's own words.
Rank each one by prevalence, severity, and how early it blocks progress.
Resolve the earliest fatal objection before asking for deeper attention.
Resolve the highest financial or reputational risk before the final commitment.
Place local objections next to the claim, feature, or term that creates them.
Reserve an FAQ for residual questions, not for the core case.
Use this default ordering when research offers no stronger sequence:

1. Fit: Is this for someone like me?
2. Outcome: Will it solve the problem I actually have?
3. Mechanism: Why should this approach work?
4. Evidence: Has it worked under relevant conditions?
5. Switching cost: What must I change, move, learn, or give up?
6. Risk: What happens if it fails or I change my mind?
7. Price: Is the return worth the total cost?
8. Procedure: What happens after I act?
Answer fit with boundaries and examples.
Answer outcome with a specific promise and result.
Answer mechanism with a compact causal explanation.
Answer evidence with attributable cases and demonstrations.
Answer switching cost with migration scope, onboarding, and time.
Answer risk with terms, support, trial structure, guarantees, or reversibility.
Answer price with decision criteria and total value.
Answer procedure with a visible next-step preview.
Never bury a purchase-blocking objection in the footer.
Do not use an FAQ to repeat marketing claims in question form.

## Place social proof where belief needs reinforcement
Use social proof to resolve a named doubt, not to announce that praise exists.
Choose sources that resemble the intended visitor in role, scale, context, or stakes.
Place a quote about implementation beside the implementation objection.
Place a result-bearing case beside the outcome claim it supports.
Place adoption evidence near perceived-risk moments.
Place authority evidence near regulated, technical, or high-trust claims.
Place a compact proof marker near the first commitment when it is verifiable.
Give every testimonial a speaker name, role, organization, and relevant context when permission allows.
Preserve the speaker's actual register.
Prefer a specific observation over generic enthusiasm.
Link to the fuller case when the decision warrants inspection.
Distinguish customer count, user count, and account count.
Date proof whose relevance changes over time.
Explain selection when a case is unusually favorable.
Never fabricate, composite, or silently rewrite a testimonial.
Do not use a logo wall as the only evidence for a substantive claim.
Do not imply endorsement beyond the permission granted.

## Build pricing around the real decision
`PERS-07` (pricing exposes the real decision) governs this section.
`PERS-09` (the price is the P0 value): on a pricing surface the price carries value-weight — larger and stronger than its labels (`DASH-06` applied to persuasion), never one row among feature rows — and every tier a visitor can choose renders a reachable action. Three-judge blind review flagged the failure identically: price set at label weight and no way for a decided buyer to act.

`PERS-10` (visual products get visual evidence): when the offer is a physical or visual object, the page develops its visual evidence within the supplied facts — the supplied depiction rendered at more than one honest view: the full object, a meaningful detail, an in-context scale reference, the per-variant states. Missing photography is a constraint to design around, not a void to leave; three blind judges independently called a single lone rendering of a visual product "thin evidence for a considered purchase." The `HON` boundary holds throughout: generic context (a wall, a hand, a room) is instantiation in the sense of `HON-03`; invented product attributes are fabrication. A geometry spec rendered as a detail crop, a framed in-room view at stated dimensions, and one swatch per variant answers the doubt the photograph would have answered — using only what was supplied.

`PERS-11` (the argument survives collapse): the resting state of a persuasion surface carries its own case. Before any hover, expansion, or navigation, each offered item presents its claim plus at least one decisive supplied evidence element — scope, constraint, outcome, metric, or mechanism. Collapse and progressive disclosure defer depth, never the case itself: a card that shows only a name and a kicker asks the visitor to click on faith, which is the opposite of persuasion. All three blind reviewers of the second matched evaluation named this failure independently on the same surface — "the case modules hold no evidence until interacted with"; "cards expose no project content beyond the title and kicker" — while `PERS-02` adjacency was technically satisfied by a one-line kicker. Adjacency of a label is not adjacency of evidence. If the facts supply scope, constraint, and outcome, the resting card carries them; the expansion earns its click by deepening a case already made.

`PERS-12` (whole before large): when a reproduced work is offered as evidence, show it whole and legible in the first viewport before any zoomed or cropped view, and size a paired text column to the artifact's height rather than leaving the artifact cut off beside empty space — this clipped-top failure recurs across evaluation rounds as a distinct, repeatable defect class.

Two portfolio builds made the contrast concrete. The losing shape ran a five-line manifesto headline the full height of the first viewport, so the only visible work was the black top edge of the first poster underneath it; a viewer sees no actual work above the fold. The passing shape put the first poster, whole and legible, in the first viewport beside a text column sized to the poster's height, with the manifesto reduced to one or two lines above it or a kicker beside it — the argument survives because the work is the argument, not a promise of work to scroll toward. The test: at the scenario viewport, without scrolling, is one reproduced work shown whole with its title legible?

`PERS-13` (the offer set is visible before the argument): on a pricing or plan-selection surface, every plan's name and price is visible in the first viewport regardless of the dealt framing, and any objection or calculation that precedes the offer set organizes around it rather than displacing it above the fold — the fold hiding the offer set recurs as the named failure across evaluation rounds, so the framing stays legal and only its position moves.

Define the decision the visitor is actually making.
Frame it as more than “which column”: choose whether to adopt, which scope fits, what risk is acceptable, and what future constraint matters.
Name the buyer, usage pattern, or operating condition each plan serves.
Make differences legible at the row where they matter.
Use stable criteria across plans so comparison stays horizontal.
Surface total cost drivers before fine print.
Explain units, limits, overages, renewal terms, and billing cadence in plain language.
Show the path for visitors whose needs exceed the table.
### Make comparison decisive
Lead with the three to seven criteria that change plan choice.
Group secondary details under meaningful decision themes.
Use exact quantities where limits drive value.
Translate technical entitlements into operational consequences.
Mark the recommended plan with a stated fit reason.
Let visitors inspect every difference without decoding checkmark patterns.
Keep plan names distinct and interpretable.
Place shared features outside the comparison when repetition obscures differences.
Support annual and monthly views without moving the decision criteria.
State savings with the actual basis and period.
### Anchor honestly
Anchor against a genuine alternative, prior cost, or larger scope.
Explain why a premium tier costs more through added outcome, capacity, service, or risk transfer.
Use a recommended tier when evidence supports a common best fit.
Show an annual total beside monthly equivalents when commitment length matters.
Make discounts visibly conditional.
Let a lower-cost path remain dignified and complete for its intended user.
Never create a decoy plan whose only job is to make another price look reasonable.
Do not hide mandatory fees, material limits, renewal changes, or cancellation terms.
Do not label a plan “most popular” without current evidence.

## Make one primary commitment
`PERS-01` (single primary commitment): choose one primary commitment per surface.
Express it as the smallest honest action that advances the visitor's decision.
Match commitment weight to confidence earned by that point.
Use “View the work” before “Hire me” when evaluation must precede contact.
Use “See plans” before “Start trial” when price fit remains unresolved.
Use “Start free trial” when terms, effort, and next steps are already clear.
Use “Book a consultation” when qualification and mutual fit are part of the product.
Repeat the same primary commitment at natural decision points.
Keep its label, destination, and promise consistent.
Let secondary paths answer genuine alternate intent, such as documentation, case studies, or contact.
Style secondary paths with lower contrast, lower area, or later placement.
Route hesitant visitors toward more evidence rather than a competing conversion.
Preview what happens after the click.
Preserve context when the commitment opens a form, checkout, or scheduler.
Never present multiple equal primary commitments on one surface.
Do not change CTA labels merely to create variety.
Do not use a low-commitment label for a high-commitment consequence.

## Make every section persuade
`PERS-06` (every section carries a belief delta): give each section a belief delta.
Name the visitor's belief before the section and the stronger belief after it.
Select one claim, one evidence form, and one next question for the section.
Make the section earn continuation, confidence, preference, or readiness.
Vary structure according to the persuasion job.
Use demonstration for mechanism, comparison for differentiation, and narrative for transformation.
Let section length follow proof burden.
End once the belief delta is achieved.
Identify filler with three tests:

1. Remove the section and ask whether the decision becomes harder.
2. Ask which objection, claim, or choice the section changes.
3. Ask what unique evidence the section contributes.
Delete sections that fail all three tests.
Merge sections that produce the same belief delta.
Rewrite sections that contain a claim but no evidence.
Promote sections that resolve a fatal objection.

## Make portfolios persuade through judgment
Lead with work that proves the type of judgment the desired client will buy.
Sequence projects to establish range only after relevance.
Present each case as situation, constraint, decision, work, and consequence.
Explain the tradeoff that made the decision difficult.
Show artifacts at a scale that permits evaluation.
Distinguish personal contribution from team contribution.
Use process material only when it reveals judgment.
State outcomes with the same evidence honesty as a product page.
Let contact follow sufficient inspection.
Never turn the portfolio into an image mosaic with no decision context.
Do not claim ownership of team outcomes without naming the role.
When the brief supplies no work artifacts, record the gap as a brief limitation and raise it — a portfolio without inspectable work cannot fully persuade. Evoke the judgment structurally in the meantime (`PERS-08`): typographic and compositional evidence built from supplied case facts, never fabricated imagery of work that does not exist (`HON-03`).

## Refuse persuade-mode AI tells
Recognize these patterns early, refuse the default, and replace each with a committed argument.
### Template Conveyor
Refuse the generic hero, three feature cards, testimonial strip, and FAQ sequence.
Build sections in the order of the visitor's actual doubts.
Give each section a distinct persuasion job and evidence form.
### Productivity Fog
Refuse the “Boost your productivity” copy register and its vague cousins.
Name who changes what, by how much, under which conditions.
Use words the buyer uses when explaining the problem internally.
### Orb Fog
Refuse decorative gradient orbs used as borrowed visual interest.
Choose imagery, type, color, and composition that embody the product's specific mechanism or character.
Spend visual emphasis on proof and consequence.
### Dashboard Theater
Refuse fake dashboard screenshots and impossible product composites.
Show the real product, a faithful prototype, or a clearly labeled conceptual view.
Annotate the exact behavior that supports the claim.
### Borrowed Trust
Refuse invented logos, anonymous praise, and implied customer relationships.
Use attributable proof, honest operating facts, or direct demonstrations.
Let absence of customer proof increase the quality of product proof.
### Countdown Fiction
Refuse unearned urgency, fake inventory, and reset-on-refresh countdowns.
Use real deadlines, capacity limits, or price changes only when their basis is clear.
Create momentum through consequence and readiness, not pressure theater.

## Final audit
Confirm that one belief and one commitment govern the surface.
Confirm that the first viewport names the offer, consequence, proof, and direction.
Confirm that every major claim has adjacent evidence.
Confirm that objections appear in decision-cost order.
Confirm that social proof is attributable, relevant, and honestly placed.
Confirm that pricing exposes the criteria, total cost drivers, and terms that change choice.
Confirm that secondary paths remain visibly subordinate.
Confirm that every section produces a belief delta.
Confirm that the work can survive removal of decorative effects.
Confirm that all six named AI tells have been refused or deliberately transformed.
